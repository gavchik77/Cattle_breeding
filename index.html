🐄 Cattle Breeding & Genetic Resilience Lab · v1.4

Two breeders. One common starting herd. Different breeding decisions. What happens to milk performance, genetic variation, and survival when a new disease or heatwave arrives?

The question

Breeder A · Production-first

Assignment: achieve Milk Yield SI 86–100 and Milk Solids SI 80–100 by generation 12. Your index places 60% weight on yield and 40% on solids. What happens to resilience?

Breeder B · Balanced breeding

Assignment: achieve Balanced Herd SI 70–80 by generation 12. The index combines milk yield, solids, disease resistance, heat tolerance, docility and low maintenance. Retain genetic diversity while improving performance.

An unexpected challenge

After several generations, expose both herds to a disease outbreak, heatwave, or combined challenge. Compare what survives.

Both herds start from the same individual animals. Outcomes are stochastic: production-focused breeding does not inevitably eliminate resistance. Model coefficients are educational assumptions, not measured cattle effect sizes.

How the genes work

Chromosome 14 · teaching linkage

M/m is a simplified production marker inspired by DGAT1 (milk fat traits) on bovine chromosome 14. D/d is an illustrative nearby resistance-linked marker, not a named bovine gene. Their assumed association is intentionally constructed for a linkage experiment. Q/q models milk solids independently.

Other trait loci

H/h contributes heat tolerance; S/s to body size; T/t to docility; C/c to low-maintenance fitness. Each animal inherits two copies per locus. Effects are simplified and additive.

Selection vs drift

Selection changes which animals become parents. Small numbers of breeding parents cause genetic drift. An approximate effective population size is displayed using the numbers of selected male and female parents. Linkage can pull a nearby allele along with a selected allele.

The initial M–D haplotypes deliberately have negative linkage disequilibrium. This does not assert that DGAT1 is linked to a real disease-resistance gene. The real bovine BoLA immune region is on chromosome 23, not 14. Traits are polygenic and environmentally influenced.

Chromosome 14: hypothetical linkage experiment

For this hypothetical chromosome 14 teaching segment, the M and D markers are placed together. D is not BoLA or a verified linked resistance gene. The starting population is deliberately enriched for Md (high milk, low resistance) and mD (lower milk, higher resistance) haplotypes. This is an assumed starting association, not a universal cattle relationship.

Try 0%, 5% and 50% recombination.

At 0%, the M and D alleles on a chromosome travel together. At 50%, the loci segregate as if unlinked. Selection can change nearby allele frequencies through linkage disequilibrium, although the effect is stochastic and not guaranteed.

Real bovine chromosome examples — separate from the simplified model

Chromosome 14

DGAT1 is associated with milk fat yield/composition. PLAG1 is associated with stature and growth. This does not imply the same allele affects both traits.

Chromosome 23

The BoLA / MHC region contributes to antigen presentation and adaptive immunity. Disease resistance is not determined by a single dominant “immunity gene”.

Chromosomes 4, 20, 27 and X

Published studies report feed-efficiency, production or disease-susceptibility associations in these regions. A QTL association is not proof of a single causal gene or a particular linkage relationship.

Other examples: LEP on chromosome 4 (appetite and metabolism); GHR on chromosome 20 (growth hormone receptor). GHR is not on chromosome 14. The model uses illustrative loci rather than claiming these named genes determine the numerical SI scores.

Starting herd · generation 0

Both breeders begin with the same individual cattle and identical starting selection indexes (SI). Index units are relative teaching scores, not industry EBVs or measured litres, percentages of milk solids, or published national SIs.

Simulation settings

Both breeders have targets at generation 12. A: Milk Yield SI ≥86 and Milk Solids SI ≥80. B: Balanced SI ≥70 (stretch target 80). The two programmes have preset breeding indexes. A uses a production index: milk yield 60%, milk solids 40%. B uses a broader index: milk yield 25%, milk solids 20%, disease resistance 15%, heat tolerance 15%, docility 15%, low maintenance 10%. The sliders select approximately that percentage of the herd as parents, including both sexes; at least one male and one female are always required.

Questions for students

  1. Did breeder A achieve a higher milk-potential index than breeder B? By how much?
  2. Which herd retained more disease-resistance allele D and heat-tolerance allele H?
  3. Did the resistance allele always decline under production-first breeding? Why might the outcome differ between runs?
  4. Repeat with 8 versus 40 breeding parents. What changes, and why?
  5. Change recombination from 0% to 50%. How does linkage affect the response?
  6. Apply the disease challenge. Compare observed mortality and survivor proportions and discuss mechanisms, limitations and other disease-control measures.
  7. Can a breeding programme improve both production and resilience? What trade-offs are visible in your results?

Scientific context and limitations

FAO emphasises that livestock genetic diversity supports adaptation to changing climates and disease threats, and that sustainable breeding objectives should account for production environments, health and long-term genetic resources. The simulation illustrates those principles, not a prediction for any specific cattle breed or pathogen.

FAO: Second Global Assessment of Animal Genetic Resources · FAO: Breeding Strategies for Sustainable Management · FAO: Animal Genetic Resources and Adaptation

This model uses seven illustrative loci, simplistic trait effects, finite-population Mendelian inheritance, and illustrative challenge-mortality probabilities, not validated epidemiological estimates. The displayed effective size uses the elementary sex-ratio formula 4NmNf/(Nm+Nf), based on selected parents, and is only an approximation; unequal parental contributions make true Ne smaller. It does not simulate real pathogens, measured genetic correlations, accurate inbreeding coefficients, realistic milk units, age structure, or genomic estimated breeding values. Heterozygosity is the fraction of heterozygous observed genotypes at the seven represented loci.
