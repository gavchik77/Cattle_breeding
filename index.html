🐄 Cattle Breeding & Genetic Resilience Lab

Two breeders. One common starting herd. Different breeding decisions. What happens to milk performance, genetic variation, and survival when a new disease or heatwave arrives?

The question

Breeder A · Production-first

Strong preference for milk, body size and docility. A small group of elite breeding parents may be used repeatedly.

Breeder B · Balanced breeding

Selects for milk and behaviour, while also considering disease resistance, heat tolerance and breeding diversity.

An unexpected challenge

After several generations, expose both herds to a disease outbreak, heatwave, or combined challenge. Compare what survives.

Both herds start from the same individual animals. Outcomes are stochastic: production-focused breeding does not inevitably eliminate resistance. Model coefficients are educational assumptions, not measured cattle effect sizes.

How the genes work

Production chromosome

Two linked loci: M/m for milk potential and D/d for disease resistance. A chromosome carries a haplotype such as MD or Md. Recombination can separate these alleles.

Other genes

H/h contributes heat tolerance; S/s to body size; T/t to docility. Each animal inherits two copies per locus. Effects are simplified and additive.

Selection vs drift

Selection changes which animals become parents. Small numbers of breeding parents cause genetic drift. Linkage can pull a nearby allele along with a selected allele.

The initial haplotypes deliberately make high milk and disease-resistance alleles partly opposed (negative linkage disequilibrium). Adjusting recombination helps reveal why linkage matters. Real milk yield, resistance and tolerance are generally polygenic and environmentally influenced.

Simulation settings

The two programmes have preset breeding indexes. A emphasises milk (65%), size (25%), docility (10%). B considers milk (30%), size (10%), docility (10%), disease resistance (25%) and heat tolerance (25%). Number of breeding parents is shared across males and females, with separate selected pools.

Questions for students

  1. Did breeder A achieve a higher milk-potential index than breeder B? By how much?
  2. Which herd retained more disease-resistance allele D and heat-tolerance allele H?
  3. Did the resistance allele always decline under production-first breeding? Why might the outcome differ between runs?
  4. Repeat with 8 versus 40 breeding parents. What changes, and why?
  5. Change recombination from 0% to 50%. How does linkage affect the response?
  6. Apply the disease challenge. Compare survivor proportions and discuss mechanisms, limitations and other disease-control measures.
  7. Can a breeding programme improve both production and resilience? What trade-offs are visible in your results?

Scientific context and limitations

FAO emphasises that livestock genetic diversity supports adaptation to changing climates and disease threats, and that sustainable breeding objectives should account for production environments, health and long-term genetic resources. The simulation illustrates those principles, not a prediction for any specific cattle breed or pathogen.

FAO: Second Global Assessment of Animal Genetic Resources · FAO: Breeding Strategies for Sustainable Management · FAO: Animal Genetic Resources and Adaptation

This model uses five illustrative loci, simplistic trait effects, finite-population Mendelian inheritance, and a conceptual stress-survival function. It does not simulate real pathogens, measured genetic correlations, accurate inbreeding coefficients, realistic milk units, age structure, or genomic estimated breeding values. Heterozygosity is the fraction of heterozygous observed genotypes at the five represented loci.
